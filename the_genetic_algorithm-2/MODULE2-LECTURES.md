Welcome to our second lecture on genetic algorithms. Now that we've learned how to encode solutions into chromosomes, we need to evolve them. It is inspired by Darwinian evolution, where fitter, more capable individuals are more likely to pass on their genes to the next generation. Of course, in order to be able to select individuals, we must be able to compare them. As such, we need some sort of function that evaluates each individual and gives us a score for how good of a solution it is. Since such a function gives us a measure of how fit an individual is, it is called a fitness function. Thus, each chromosome in the population is evaluated by this fitness function. The more fit a solution is, the higher its probability of being selected for reproduction. However, fitter doesn't necessarily mean perfect. We still want some randomness so that we don't miss good solutions hidden behind less fit individuals. Now, there are several commonly used selection strategies, and today we will discuss four of them. Top case selection, roulette wheel selection, tournament selection, and finally, rank base selection. Each of these methods have trade-offs in diversity and convergence speed. Let's take a look at each one of them in detail. We begin with top K selection since it is, for sure, the most basic and intuitive method. Here, we simply order all individuals in descending order according to their fitness and the top K individuals are selected for reproduction. Though it is a very simple and intuitive selection method, it is not as commonly used, mostly due to its lack of randomness. This lack of randomness translates into a lack of diversity, which can be detrimental in the long run. The algorithm may get caught in a local minimum and fail to explore promising areas of the solution space that may be hidden behind less fit individuals. As such, in order to introduce some randomness into the selection process, we can use Fitness Proportionate Selection, also known as Roulette Wheel Selection. It's a probabilistic selection method weighted by fitness. It assigns each individual a slice of a roulette wheel proportional to its fitness. So, if an individual has 30% of the total fitness, it gets 30% of the wheel. Then, we spin the wheel to select who gets to reproduce. Over many spins, fitter individuals get selected more. But a key characteristic of this selection strategy is that lower fitness ones will still have a chance, which helps maintain generic diversity. Here's an example of roulette wheel selection. We have three chromosomes, with fitness scores 4, 10, and 6, respectively. The total fitness for the population is then equal to 20. This means that chromosome 1 gets 20% chance of being selected. it. Chromosome 2 has a 50% chance, and chromosome 3 gets the remaining 30%. We can stack together the probabilities of all of these individuals as we see in this picture on the left. Then, when we need to select a new individual, we can use a random number generator to generate a number between, say, 0 and 1. If the number generated, for example, is between 0 and 0.2, then chromosome 1 would get selected. If instead the number is between 0.2 and 0.7, C2 would get selected. These problems have led to the development of alternative selection methods, like tournament and rank-based selection, which we will look at next. Tournament selection is basically a hybrid between random and top-case selection. We randomly select a few individuals, say, three or four, and pick the best among them. Then, we repeat this process as many times as candidates are needed. It is simple, efficient, and doesn't require normalization or scaling. Furthermore, this method gives us more control over selection pressure. That is, we can favor a more strict selection process with bigger tournament sizes, or make the selection closer to a random one with smaller sizes. Lastly, we have rank-based selection, which addresses the main issues with roulette selection. Let's wrap up this lesson by directly comparing all the methods we just discussed. TOP-K is definitely the most straightforward selection method, but its key disadvantage is that it does not involve any randomness, which may lead to early convergence and trap the algorithm at a local minimum. Roulette selection is easy to implement, but can be unstable as very fit individuals tend to dominate the selection process. Tournament, on the other hand, gives us more control over selection pressure by adjusting the tournament sizes. In this lecture, we will explore how genetic algorithms behave over time. We will look at how convergence happens, why it might stall, and what can we do to guide the process. Convergence refers to how, after a number of iterations, the population settles around one or more solutions. Initially, in the early generations, both the best and average fitness levels increase rapidly. This signifies that the algorithm is quickly discovering better solutions. However, as the generations progress, diversity decreases and the rate of improvement slows down. Here, the fitness levels begin to plateau. This flattening indicates that the algorithm has likely converged. This means that it has found an optimal solution, or near optimal solution, and further generations are unlikely to produce significantly better results. The population has become more uniform, with most individuals being very similar to the best solution found. Convergence is a common pitfall in genetic algorithms. This phenomenon usually occurs due to a loss of genetic diversity. One or a few pretty good solutions might dominate the population too early. As a result, all the individuals in the population become very similar, and without a diverse range of genes to work with, the algorithm loses its ability to explore other parts of the solution space. It has become trapped at a local optima. ********* convergence can be caused by using a selection strategy with high selection pressure and poorly tuned mutation and crossover rates. You'll notice, while the graph on this slide looks similar to the previous graph, there's a critical difference. convergence. In this case, the fitness flattens out very early in the run, long before reaching the 100th generation. More importantly, it has plateaued at a lower fitness level compared with the previous example of a healthy convergence. Convergence highlights the importance of maintaining a diverse population throughout the generic algorithm life cycle. There is a delicate balance between trying new things, also known as exploration, and refining what works, also known as exploitation. High diversity means better exploration, but possibly slower convergence. Some things we can do to improve population diversity are a suitable mutation and crossover rates, and using a different selection method with a lower selection pressure. Another alternative is fitness sharing, which encourages diversity by penalizing individuals that are too similar to others. In it, chromosomes share their fitness with nearby neighbors, and their individual's fitness is adjusted based on how crowded their area of the solution space is. Finally, we have elitism. Elitism ensures that the best solutions are not lost during selection and reproduction. A small number of the top performing individuals are carried over unchanged to the next generation. These individuals bypass mutation and crossover for that generation. This avoids the risk of good genetic material being lost too early. Combining elitism with diversity -preserving mechanisms ensures strong solutions are retained while the rest of the population continues exploring. However, if overused, elitism can greatly reduce diversity and lead to convergence. Let's finish up our lecture with a quick summary comparing high versus low diversity strategies in generic algorithms. A high diversity strategy prioritizes variety in the population. This allows broader exploration and helps the algorithm escape local optima, making it more robust for complex problems. However, this comes at the cost of slower convergence, since more generations are needed due to the wider surge. On the other hand, a low diversity strategy focuses the surge in a narrow area. This results in faster convergence, which is great if the landscape is simple or unimodal, but increases the risk of getting stuck in local optima or plateaus. Your choice between these strategies should depend on the problem landscape and how much computation time you're willing to trade for solution quality.
